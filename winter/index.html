Signals: what to expect in your county

A signal is the impact level a named storm is expected to bring to one county, issued once the storm has a name. It does not change with lead time: Signal 3 three days out means the same as Signal 3 during the storm. Signals are issued only for counties inside a named storm's expected footprint.

Impact level thresholds

Each hazard gets a level from 1 to 5. The county's impact level, issued as its signal inside a named storm, is the highest of snow, ice and wind. It goes up one step when two of those reach Level 3, or when wind chills reach Level 3 on top of snow or ice at Level 2 or higher. Cold never sets the signal on its own, and wind can rate at most one level above the snow. A Super Blizzard is always Signal 5.

Storm category W1 to W5

The category rates the whole storm from the impacts it is expected to cause. Every county in the footprint adds its impact level, weighted by people and land affected: weight × (0.6 × population ÷ 1 million + 0.4 × area ÷ 50,000 km²), with weights 1, 3, 9, 27, 81 for Levels 1 to 5. The total is the WSC Storm Index.

CategoryStorm IndexWhat it means

Examples: 4 in of snow around Tulsa scores about 1 (W1) and is named, since Minor impacts cover about 1.4 million people. A 20 in Montana blizzard scores about 65 (W3). The NWS warnings in effect the evening before the 2011 Groundhog Day Blizzard score about 1,400 (W5). A 16 in Chicago blizzard with an ice band scores about 240 (W5).

Naming

Every run rates the expected impact in every county, Level 1 Minor to Level 5 Extreme, whether or not a storm has a name. A storm is named when its forecast footprint, within 72 hours and touching the lower 48, meets all three: Storm Index 1 or more (W1), at least 250,000 people expecting impacts (Level 1 Minor or worse), and at least 10,000 km² (about four average counties) expecting impacts. One county alone can never trigger a name. Once a storm is named, the impact levels inside its footprint are issued as signals. It keeps its name from run to run as long as its footprint overlaps the previous one, and the name retires for the season when no impacts remain in its footprint. Impacted areas that do not qualify are shown as monitored areas, outlined on the map, with no signals. Alaska and Hawaii systems are monitored but not named.

Advisories

Every named storm gets advisories each run. Neighboring counties with the same signal and hazards share one segment, with what to expect, when (local time), impacts for each hazard and what to do. Headlines are added for Ice Storm (ice Level 3 or higher), Blizzard (gusts 35 mph or higher with snow Level 2 or higher) and Super Blizzard conditions.

Special designation

Super Blizzard

A storm that ties or breaks a county's 24-hour snowfall record. It is a designation, not a level of its own: any county where a Super Blizzard is forecast goes straight to Signal 5, and its advisory carries a Super Blizzard headline.

Nationwide, each county's threshold is the median of its own NOAA NCEI 1-day snowfall record and its neighbors' records, so one unusually low or high station record cannot swing a county by itself. The 12 counties with no record borrow the nearest county's. The house thresholds below override NOAA for these metros. NOAA lists Houston at 19 in (February 1895), so the 5 in house rule for Harris County is far stricter than the record.

Naming

Storm names

Names come from three rotating lists. When a season uses every name on its list, naming continues on the Auxiliary list. Alaska and Hawaii systems are monitored but not named.

How counties are grouped into storms: each storm is built around a core of touching counties at Level 2 or higher whose peaks fall within 18 hours of each other. Level 1 counties attach to the nearest core. Two pieces join into one storm when they touch or sit one county apart and the counties along their shared edge peak within 18 hours of each other. A storm sweeping across the country stays one storm, while a separate system that only brushes against it, like the Pacific Northwest snow during Uri in February 2021, gets its own footprint.
